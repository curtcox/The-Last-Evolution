# Campbell and the pulps

*Draft for Round 2. The site's notes on who wrote the story, where it appeared, and what became of them. Sources are in
[refs.yaml](refs.yaml).*

## *Amazing Stories*
Hugo Gernsback launched *Amazing Stories* in April 1926. It was the first magazine given entirely to what Gernsback
called "scientifiction" ([Amazing Stories][wp-amazing]). He lost it to bankruptcy in 1929, and from then until 1938 it
was edited by T. O'Conor Sloane, a scientist and inventor already in his late seventies ([T. O'Conor
Sloane][wp-sloane]). In 1932 it was still a large "bedsheet" magazine, 8½ by 11¾ inches, and it shrank to pulp size the
next year.

The August 1932 issue ([scan][archive-1932]) gave "The Last Evolution" eight pages, from 414 to 421, with an editor's
introduction:
> It must be the dream of countless numbers to reach the acme of evolution

That's the opening of a longer sentence wondering who would give up their "human weaknesses" to get there. It's on the
story's title page ([page 414](../assets/ref/amazing_1932-08_p414.jpg)), but not in the reprint our text comes from.

## John W. Campbell, Jr.
Campbell was born in Newark, New Jersey, on 8 June 1910 ([SFE][sfe-campbell]). His first story, "When the Atoms Failed",
appeared in *Amazing* in January 1930, while he was a student. His very first sale was lost when Sloane mislaid the
manuscript. His early fame came from the Arcot, Morey and Wade stories: super-science adventures with ever-bigger
battles and weapons.

"The Last Evolution" came out in 1932, the year he took his physics degree, and he was 22. It's a different kind of
story from his space operas: a memoir told by a machine, with an elegy in it. Sam Moskowitz, introducing the 1961
reprint ([scan][archive-1961]), tells how that happened. Campbell had first written "Twilight", a story of mankind's
last remnants tended by faithful machines, and every editor turned it down as too far from his usual kind of story. He
wrote "The Last Evolution" in its place. Moskowitz calls it the transition between Campbell's super-science and the
"mood" stories still to come, and he counts it among Campbell's better short stories. Two years later, as "Don A.
Stuart", he published "Twilight" (*Astounding*, November 1934), a mood piece about humanity fading while its machines
run on ([Twilight][wp-twilight]). It was later chosen for *The Science Fiction Hall of Fame*.

## The editor
In September 1937 Street & Smith made Campbell editor of *Astounding*, and he kept the job for 34 years, until he died
in 1971 ([SFE][sfe-campbell]). He shaped the period fans call the Golden Age of science fiction, finding and pushing
writers such as Isaac Asimov and Robert A. Heinlein ([Golden Age of Science Fiction][wp-golden-age]). He published
"Who Goes There?" (1938), the basis of the *Thing* films, under the Stuart name ([Who Goes There?][wp-who-goes-there]).

His later record is darker. He promoted pseudoscience, including psychic powers and L. Ron Hubbard's Dianetics, which
was launched in *Astounding*. His editorials took a right-wing line, and in his later years he struggled to tolerate
dissent ([SFE][sfe-campbell]). In August 2019 the writer Jeannette Ng, accepting the award then named for him, called
him a fascist. That month the publisher of *Analog*, *Astounding*'s successor, renamed it the Astounding Award for Best
New Writer ([Astounding Award][wp-astounding-award]). The site's notes will present this as history, alongside the
story, not instead of it.

## Leo Morey
The illustration is by Leo Morey, the working name of Leopoldo Morey y Peña, born in Lima, Peru, on 24 October 1889
([SFE][sfe-morey]). He studied engineering at Louisiana State University and drew for newspapers before Sloane hired him
in 1929. Sloane needed a replacement for Frank R. Paul, who had left with Gernsback. Morey painted 71 covers for
*Amazing* and 12 for *Amazing Stories Quarterly*, and later worked for other pulps and for comics. He died in 1965.

His picture for this story shows the moment of F-2's birth: F-1's tentacles, the small torpedo of metal in its aura, and
a man shielding his eyes. It's on [the Read page](https://curtcox.github.io/The-Last-Evolution/read/#illustration), and
in the film at that moment.

## The reprint, and the text we use
*Amazing* reprinted the story in March 1961 as "A Classic Reprint", under the editor Cele Goldsmith ([Amazing
Stories][wp-amazing], [scan][archive-1961]). Project Gutenberg transcribed that reprint ([eBook #27462][gutenberg]), and
the film reads it with 16 readings from the 1932 printing restored. Comparing the three texts turned up a 24-word clause
and two negatives lost in 1961, a dozen smaller typesetting slips, and a misprint from 1932 that Gutenberg corrected:
F-2 for F-1, three times ([the comparison](../text/comparison_1932.md)).

Neither printing's copyright was renewed: *Amazing*'s renewals begin with later issues
([first periodical renewals][penn-renewals]). The story and Morey's illustration are in the public domain in the US.
