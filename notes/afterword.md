# After the end

*The afterword to the annotated film, shown below it on the watch page, and scrolled to when the film ends. Draft for
Round 7. Sources are in [refs.yaml](refs.yaml).*

The record ends by handing the argument to you: "So, you who have read, believe as you will. Then think—and maybe, you
will change your belief." Here's some of what happened after it was written, to think with.

## What became of Campbell

Two years later, as "Don A. Stuart", Campbell published "Twilight" (*Astounding*, November 1934), the story he'd
written first and couldn't sell: mankind dwindled and idle, its machines running on perfectly, without purpose
([Twilight][wp-twilight]). *The Last Evolution* is the triumphant version, and "Twilight" the sad one.

In 1937 he became editor of *Astounding*, and he kept the job for 34 years, until he died in 1971. He found and pushed
writers such as Isaac Asimov and Robert A. Heinlein, and shaped what fans call the Golden Age of science fiction
([Golden Age][wp-golden-age]). His later record is darker. He promoted pseudoscience, Dianetics among it, and his
editorials took a hard right-wing line ([SFE][sfe-campbell]). In 2019 the award named for him was renamed the Astounding
Award ([Astounding Award][wp-astounding-award]). That history sits alongside the story, not in place of it.

## What became of the science

The positron was photographed the month the story appeared, and it settled Roal's question the other way. Matter
annihilates only with antimatter, an electron with a positron, and a proton and an electron never do
([positron][wp-positron], [annihilation][wp-annihilation]). The hydrogen "wall" turned out to be the atom's lowest
state: there's nothing below it to fall to ([Bohr model][wp-bohr]). The first electronic memories came in the late 1940s
([Williams tube][wp-williams-tube]), and the machines that think are still being argued about.

## What became of the idea

Campbell wasn't first. Samuel Butler wondered in 1863 whether machines might evolve to supplant us
([Darwin among the Machines][wp-butler]). After him came Turing in 1951, expecting that the machines would "take
control" ([Quote Investigator][qi-turing]); I. J. Good's "intelligence explosion" in 1965 ([technological
singularity][wp-singularity]); and Hans Moravec's *Mind Children* in 1988, robots as humanity's heirs ([Mind
Children][wp-mind-children]).

Today the argument has two camps. One says succession is coming and needn't be feared; in 2025 the AI pioneer Richard
Sutton called it inevitable ([Dwarkesh Podcast][dwarkesh-sutton]). The other says it's a risk to be prevented; in 2023
hundreds of researchers and the heads of the major AI labs signed a one-sentence statement that the risk of extinction
from AI should be a global priority ([Statement on AI Risk][cais-statement]).

Campbell's story fits neither neatly. Its machines are loyal, they grieve, and they fight for their makers, and mankind
is killed from outside. What it asks is the question Roal answers and F-2 hands back: if the heirs are better, should we
mind?

## What became of the text

*Amazing* reprinted the story in March 1961, and that's the text Project Gutenberg transcribed
([eBook #27462][gutenberg]). Setting it beside the 1932 pages turned up a 24-word clause and two negatives lost in 1961,
and a dozen smaller slips. The film reads the 1932 readings, and [the Read page](/read/) marks each one. Neither
printing's copyright was renewed, so the story and Morey's picture are free for anyone to use in the US
([first periodical renewals][penn-renewals]).
