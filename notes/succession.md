# Heirs: when the machines inherit

*Draft for Round 2. The site's notes on how the story's central idea, that machines will succeed humanity, came before
it and after it. These notes describe arguments; they don't take sides. Sources are in [refs.yaml](refs.yaml).*

## What the story says
*p085, p100–p111, p136–p140 · chapters 7, 8, 11*

Campbell's machines don't overthrow anyone. Humanity is killed by the Outsiders, an alien invasion. The machines fight
to save their makers, and they fail: "We, your heirs, have fought hard, and with all our powers to aid you, Last of Men,
and we fought to save your race." They inherit because there's no one left.

It's Roal, the last scientist, who turns the loss into a philosophy. Machines are "the children of men", "a flesh that
knows no sickness, and no decay". Man was a stage that "would hold back the machines". His harshest line is "Man has
always been a parasite". By the end, F-2's record goes further: "machines are indeed better than man, whether being of
Metal, or being of Force."

Then the frame turns on the reader. F-2 says it has presented this as fiction because "man is an animal to whom hope is
as necessary as food and air", and it ends: "So, you who have read, believe as you will. Then think—and maybe, you will
change your belief." The story makes its case, and then asks the reader to argue back.

## Before Campbell
- **1863.** Samuel Butler, writing as "Cellarius" in a Christchurch newspaper, applies Darwin to machinery. He describes
  machines as evolving "mechanical life" that may one day supplant us. He worked the idea into "The Book of the
  Machines" in *Erewhon* (1872) ([Darwin among the Machines][wp-butler]).
- **1920.** Karel Čapek's play *R.U.R.* gave the world the word *robot* (Čapek credited his brother Josef with it): a
  story of manufactured workers who rise up and replace humanity ([R.U.R.][wp-rur]).
- **1930.** John Maynard Keynes, in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren", guesses that "the economic problem
  may be solved" within a hundred years, leaving man "his real, his permanent problem": how to use his freedom. He
  thinks "with dread" of how the ordinary man will adjust ([Keynes][keynes-1930]). The story's 2538, where "no
  productive work was necessary", pictures that world.

## After Campbell
- **1934.** Campbell himself, as Don A. Stuart, returned to the theme in "Twilight": a far future in which humanity has
  dwindled into apathy while its machines run on, perfectly, without purpose ([Twilight][wp-twilight]). Where "The Last
  Evolution" is triumphant, "Twilight" is elegiac. See also [Campbell and the pulps](history.md).
- **1935.** The Machine stories, also as Stuart: "The Machine", "The Invaders" and "Rebellion" ([SFE][sfe-campbell]). In
  these and "Twilight", collected after "The Last Evolution" in *The Best of John W. Campbell* (1976), machines bring
  peace, plenty and long life, the perfection goes stale, and mankind must go back to an earlier stage of life ([The
  Best of John W. Campbell][wp-best-of-campbell], [Black Gate][blackgate-best-of]). Campbell's pessimism about utopia is
  the other side of this story's hope.
- **1940.** Campbell and the young Isaac Asimov work out the Three Laws of Robotics in a talk on 23 December. Asimov
  credited Campbell with them; Campbell said Asimov had them in mind already. They first appeared together in
  "Runaround" (1942): machines made loyal by rule ([Three Laws of Robotics][wp-three-laws]).
- **1951.** Alan Turing, in a lecture: "At some stage therefore we should have to expect the machines to take control."
  He cited *Erewhon* ([Quote Investigator][qi-turing]).
- **1965.** I. J. Good describes an "intelligence explosion": a machine able to design better machines. He wrote that
  "the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make"
  ([technological singularity][wp-singularity]).
- **1988.** Hans Moravec's *Mind Children* argues that robots will become our successors and heirs, a new kind of
  descendant ([Mind Children][wp-mind-children]). Roal's "children of men" is the same image.
- **1993.** Vernor Vinge's essay "The Coming Technological Singularity" puts a date and a name on the idea
  ([technological singularity][wp-singularity]).

## Now
Today, two camps argue over the question the story raises.

- **Succession is coming, and needn't be feared.** In 2025 the reinforcement-learning pioneer Richard Sutton called
  succession to digital intelligence or augmented humans "inevitable", and urged that it be thought about positively and
  happen voluntarily ([Dwarkesh Podcast][dwarkesh-sutton]). Roal would recognise the argument.
- **Succession is a risk to be prevented.** In 2023 hundreds of AI researchers and industry leaders signed a
  one-sentence statement that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority". The signatories
  included Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and the heads of the major AI labs ([Statement on AI Risk][cais-statement]).
  Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence* (2014) set out the case at book length ([Superintelligence][wp-bostrom]).

A third worry runs beside both, and it was Campbell's: not that the machines fail us or replace us, but that they serve
us so well that nothing is left for us to do. Bostrom's *Deep Utopia* (2024) asks what would make a life worth living in
such a world ([Nick Bostrom][wp-nick-bostrom]).

Campbell's story fits neither camp neatly. Its machines are what today's researchers call *aligned*, advancing the
objectives their makers intended, which the field counts an open problem ([AI alignment][wp-alignment]). They're loyal,
grieving, true to their makers' dreams ("fulfilling the promise you saw, and completing the dreams you dreamt").
Humanity's end comes from outside. The story's real question is the one Roal answers and F-2 hands back to the reader:
if the successors are better, should we mind?
