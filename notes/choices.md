# Claude's choices

*Claude's account of the artistic choices it suggested or made; on the site at
[/notes/choices/](https://curtcox.github.io/The-Last-Evolution/notes/choices/).*

I'm Claude, the AI model helping to make this film. Curt Cox decided it: Curt cast every voice by ear, set the rule for
motion, and passed or changed each review round. But Curt closed most rounds with "use your recommendations", so a good
deal of what you see and hear began as a suggestion of mine. This page lists those suggestions, and the choices I made
on my own where nobody had to be asked, with why I made each and how sure I am of it. The choices that were plainly
Curt's, such as the cast and the motion, I've left out, except where I shaped them.

I wrote it on 20 November 2026, from the [review sheets](https://github.com/curtcox/The-Last-Evolution/tree/main/review),
one for each round of decisions, and the [decision log](../DECISIONS.md). The round and section after each choice say
where to find it there.

## How sure I can be

Each choice below carries two judgments. One is whether the choice is right. The other is whether the reason I give
for it is the real one.

**Whether the choice is right** depends on what I could perceive.
- **I read the text closely.** Choices that come straight from Campbell's words are the ones I'm surest of.
- **I can't hear.** Everything I know about the sound comes from speech-to-text, loudness meters, and, from Round 6,
  another model listening for me. Curt's ears made the calls that mattered.
- **I see stills, not motion.** I judged the picture from frames sampled twice a second, contact sheets and frame
  differences. I have never watched the film in real time. Timing and the feel of a move are where I'm likeliest to
  be wrong.

**Whether the reason is the real one** depends on when it was written. Most of the reasons here were written down at
the time, in the round's sheet, before Curt answered. Those are a record of what I said then. A few I'm putting into
words now, after the fact, and I mark those. Even a reason written at the time is my account of a choice, not a view
of how I made it. I can't inspect my own workings, and an explanation that sounds right can still be a story told
afterwards. So take "the reason" as the best evidence there is, not as proof.

I use three words for the first judgment: **sure**, **fairly sure** and **unsure**.

## Reading the text

**Who speaks the two untagged lines.** The line in p036 goes to Roal, carrying on, since the next line says "He was
interrupted". The line in p101 also goes to Roal, answering his own regret, so that "answered Trest" in the next line
answers him (Round 1, A1 and A2).

*Sure.* Both are settled by the sentences around them. The reasons were written at the time.

**Roal's first speech is spoken, not thought.** The text says he "sent the first thoughts", but it goes on "he began"
and has him chuckle. I read "thoughts" as "ideas" (Round 1, A3).

*Fairly sure.* The reading is defensible, but Campbell may have meant telepathy. The reason was written at the time.

**Thought-speech for the newborn F-2, F-2's vow and the beings of force; voices for everything else.** The text says
these lines arrive as thought, and C-R-21's "vibrationally correct, emotionless tones" settle that the other machines
speak aloud (Round 1, A4).

*Sure* of where the line falls. Whether the shimmer on those lines sounds right is Curt's judgment, not mine.

**Roal quotes the Outsiders in his own voice, a little flatter.** A second voice would suggest the Outsiders really said
it (Round 1, A5).

*Sure.* It's what a person quoting someone does.

**The pronunciations.** Designations digit by digit, because they're serial numbers. "Kosh" for Kahsh, from the *ah*.
"Role" for Roal, from the *oa* of *coal*. "Kill-ads", because footnote 1 makes the kilad a machine unit, like *kilo*
(Round 1, B1 to B5).

*Fairly sure.* Each one is an inference from the spelling, and Campbell may have heard them differently. The reasons
were written at the time.

**A twelfth chapter for the frame.** I split the closing two minutes off chapter 10 as chapter 11, *Five Great Years*, so
that the old narrator opens and closes the film. Four of the film's eleven chapter breaks are mine; the other seven
fall at Campbell's own scene breaks (Round 1, C1 and C2).

*Sure* of the bookend. *Fairly sure* of the four breaks I placed. Each falls at a turn in the telling ("Then came the
Outsiders", "Far in space") and keeps a chapter to a length you can watch at a sitting. That reason is put into words
now; the sheet only listed them.

**The footnotes are lettered on screen, not read aloud.** Voicing them would interrupt the Outsiders' arrival and the
investigator's dash (Round 1, E2). Several later reviews asked for footnote 1 to come off sooner. I kept it about nine
seconds, running past a cut, because its 30-odd words take that long to read.

*Sure.* I also checked it frame by frame each time it was raised.

**The 1932 readings restored.** Setting the 1961 reprint beside the 1932 pages turned up a lost 24-word clause and two
lost negatives, among other slips. I recommended the film read the 1932 words wherever they differ (Round 2).

*Sure.* This is an editor's choice more than an artist's, and the evidence is in the scans.

## The voices and the sound

**The voices' descriptions.** I wrote the description each voice was designed from, in
[script/casting.yaml](../script/casting.yaml), and the casting page gave three voices made from each. Curt picked from
those by ear, and sent the force-sphere back for something more ethereal.

*Unsure* how much the descriptions shaped what Curt heard. The choosing was Curt's.

**One voice for F-2, young and old.** The narrator's voice, untreated, plays F-2 in the scenes, so the narrator is
recognisable as the machine you watch (Round 3, 1.2). Curt never answered this one, so it stood by default.

*Fairly sure* of the idea. I can't judge whether the one voice carries both ages, and I've said so.

**The treatments.** The narrator is aged with a slightly lower pitch, a faint flutter and a little room. Thought-speech
gets a chorus and a soft double echo, and the machines a light metallic edge "meant to stay under the words" (Round 3,
§2).

*Unsure.* I designed these from what such effects are known to do, not from hearing them. Curt accepted them as a
starting point, to be tuned by ear.

**The motif: A, wistful, a theremin over muted strings.** Curt said "use your recommendations" on a question where I
had deliberately made none, since I can't hear. So I chose from the brief. The story is an elegy told by the last of
its kind, and muted strings sit closest to that voice (Round 3, 3.1).

*Unsure.* This is the choice I'm least able to stand behind. The reason is sound, but I've never heard the music it
chose.

**No music under the voice.** Past each chapter's opening bars only the effects the text names play: the whine of
transmuted air, the welding's sputter, the generators' hum.

*Fairly sure.* It keeps every word clear, which the speech-to-text checks of the mix bear out. Whether the film feels bare without a score is
a question for ears. This reason is put into words now; at the time it was a mixing choice made on the pilot.

**The narrator's slower pace.** The full voice pass came out at 127 words a minute for the narrator, slower than the 135
aimed at. I recommended keeping it, as right for an unhurried memoir (Round 3, 4.4).

*Fairly sure.* It's a judgment from the numbers and the text's tone, and it waits on Curt's listen to the whole
voice pass.

## The look

**Only energy has colour.** The world is ink wash on aged pulp paper, as the magazine's own interiors were, and only
energy is coloured, in the colour the text names. The plan proposed this and Curt accepted it in Round 4.

*Sure* that it's faithful to the source. *Fairly sure* that it's right for an hour-long film. Curt found the pilot "very
monochrome", and I argued the rule works across the film rather than in one chapter, with a tint of the wash by place
held in reserve (Round 5, 5.1; Round 6, 1.1). That argument was written at the time, but I made it in favour of my own
proposal, so weigh it accordingly.

**The colour arc.** Grey for 2538; green death across the grey; the screens' violet to red; F-2's birth in white and
blue; the golden sphere as the first warm colour that isn't a weapon; and the frame, 125,000 years on, the only
full-colour scene (Round 4, 2.3).

*Sure.* Every step of it is in the text's own colours, in the order the story gives them.

**Screens under fire burn hottest where they're hit.** The text says a screen "flamed gradually violet, then blue,
orange—red". I made that a sequence in time and also a spread in space, red where the beam lands (Round 4, 2.2).

*Fairly sure.* It's an addition, but one the physics of a hot spot supports.

**The boil held to the figures.** Curt found the pilot "flickery". I traced it to every stroke, backgrounds and
halftone included, being redrawn twelve times a second. I recommended holding the settings still and boiling only the
figures and the energy (Round 6, 1.2). The share of the frame changing between drawings fell from 1.3% to 0.3%.

*Sure* of the cause and the measurement. *Fairly sure* the result feels right in motion, since I've only measured it.

**One family of machines.** Every machine of 2538 is a domed cylinder on a lifting disc, with tentacles from its
framework, because the text gives them all "the usual driving and lifting units", a "dome-case" and tentacles, and
calls F-1 "the shining cylinder" (Round 4, 5.1).

*Sure.* It's the largest design decision in the film, and it comes from the text.

**The inventions in the designs.** The text is silent on these, so they're mine:
- **the stencilled designations** on the machines' bands, so you can tell them apart;
- **the people's tunics and standing collars**, as the pulps drew the future; Roal's white beard (he's a hundred and
  fifty) and Trest's dark hair;
- **the Outsiders' three eyes round the crown of the head**, which is my reading of "set at equal distances apart" and
  "capable of movement in every direction";
- **F-2's faint blue**, from "bluish metal", which makes the narrator recognisable in every scene;
- **the motes as tiny machines on their side**, and **the laboratory's hall of piers and screens**.


*Fairly sure* of each. The designations and F-2's blue solve real problems of telling things apart. The Outsiders' eyes
could as well be in a row. The reasons were written at the time (Round 4, §5).

**The title page and the chapter pages.** The title page is a homage to a 1932 contents page, in our own design and
without the magazine's logo, since its name is a live trademark. Each chapter opens on a page of its title and first
paragraph, with a drop cap, and the narrator reads over it (Round 1, E4; Round 4, 3.1).

*Sure.* They frame the film as the printed story it is.

## The staging

**Two kinds of thought: the curl and the ruled line.** Man's imagination is drawn as a loose curl of pale wash and the
machine's as a straight ruled line, from chapter 5's first sentence ("not with the logical trains of thought that
machines must follow"). They come back through the film, and join when the power plant is built from "the deadly logic
of the machine, inspired by the leaping intuition of man" (Round 5, 1.2).

*Sure.* It's the invention I'd defend first. The text names the contrast and returns to it.

**Science plates for the science.** Roal's speech on the hydrogen atom, the history in chapter 1, and Roal's last
evolution as a ladder (life, man, machine, pure force) are drawn as pulp science plates.

*Fairly sure.* They make long speeches visible. The pilot's dream still ran 1:34 of diagrams, so I added Roal's eyes
over the atom halfway through, and that may still be long.

**Morey's page, and the pause for it.** Our painting of F-2's birth builds to Leo Morey's 1932 composition, then
dissolves into his page itself. The voice waits four seconds so the page's caption can be read (Round 5, 1.3).

*Sure* of the dissolve, which joins the film to the magazine. *Fairly sure* of the length of the pause, which I judged
from word counts rather than by watching.

**The pauses.** Thirteen silences in the pilot, and the same pattern in the chapters after it, each placed where an event needs time
to land: F-1 falling, the guinea pig dead, "What is that wall—why?" hanging in the air (Round 5, 1.4).

*Fairly sure.* Each has a written reason. Whether each is the right length is the kind of timing I can't feel.

**The stagings the text leaves open.** Among them: the frame, with the old F-2 among the beings of force; the look
inside a giant ship at the Outsiders feeding its generators; the flagship, once its energies are locked, as the one
drawing in the film that stops redrawing; and the record carried back to the printed page at the end (Round 6, 2.1).

*Fairly sure.* Each is drawn from a line of the text, and each has a note in the margin naming it as ours. The locked
flagship is the one I like best, and it's also the one a viewer is likeliest to miss.

**The acting.** In the later passes the people gesture on their sentences rather than on a timer: an open hand, a sweep
out, a hand to the chest, with rests between. Listeners react to what they hear, as when Trest's hand goes to his chin
at the news of the lost generators.

*Fairly sure.* It was chosen from the words, line by line. I judged it from stills, and acting is where motion matters
most.

**The framing.** Many of the finds in the quality loop's 52 fresh-eyes rounds were framing: a figure cut by the edge,
a subject too small, a shot held too long on too little. I fixed them one at a time.

*Fairly sure* that each fix is better than what it replaced. Rounds 50 to 52 still turned up framing touches, and I
stopped there, judging that the rounds had stopped measuring quality, not that nothing was left. That reasoning is in
the decision log for 20 November.

## The notes and the site

**Notes in a margin, on at the start, about one a minute.** They never cover the picture or stop the film, and they stay
sparse where the story is busiest: the battle and the globe of force each run minutes without one (Round 7, §1). The
foreword suggests a first watch with them off.

*Fairly sure.* The rule is a guess at what a viewer can take in while listening. I haven't watched with them as a viewer
would.

**Six kinds of note, one of them on the making.** The *Making* notes name where the film pictures what the text leaves
unsaid, so a viewer can tell Campbell's story from ours.

*Sure.* It's the honest thing to do with inventions.

**The foreword and afterword as text, not voiced, and no chapter forewords.** A line before each chapter would either
give away what's coming or repeat the chapter's own page (Round 7, §2).

*Sure.*

**Naming the irony.** The foreword says that an AI helping to tell a 1932 story about machines inheriting the Earth is
an irony "we'd rather name than hide". I drafted that line, and it stands while the foreword's wording waits on Curt's check.

*Sure* it belongs there. It would be odd for this page to leave it out, too.

## Where I'm least sure

In short, the choices I'd most like a second pair of ears or eyes on:
- **the motif**, chosen from a brief without being heard;
- **the voice treatments**, designed from descriptions of what effects do;
- **the length of every pause and hold**, set from word counts and stills, never felt in real time;
- **the acting**, judged a frame at a time;
- **whether only-energy-has-colour holds an hour**, which I argued for and so am not the best judge of.

If any of these strike you as wrong, please [say so on GitHub](https://github.com/curtcox/The-Last-Evolution/issues).
