Before you watch

This is John W. Campbell, Jr.'s The Last Evolution, from Amazing Stories for August 1932, read in full. Every word of it is spoken, in order, by a cast of voices, over paintings in the manner of the magazine's own ink illustrations. It runs about an hour, in twelve chapters.

The story is a machine's memoir. Its first line tells you the machine is the last of its kind; the rest tells you how it came to be, and what came after mankind.

When it was written

Campbell was 22 and finishing a physics degree (SFE). 1932 was the year the atom came apart: the neutron was found in February, the first atom split by a machine in April, and the positron, the first antimatter, photographed on 2 August, the month this story was on the stands (discovery of the neutron, positron). There was no electronic computer anywhere. When the story says "machine", it's imagining something no one had built.

Why this story

Curt Cox, who made this film, first read Campbell in the 1980s, in The Best of John W. Campbell (1976). That book opens with this story, and "Twilight", "The Machine", "The Invaders" and "Rebellion" follow it (The Best of John W. Campbell). What stayed with them was Campbell's pessimism about utopia. In those stories, machines bring peace, plenty and long life, and the perfection goes stale. Mankind, with no work left to do, has to go back to an earlier stage of life (Black Gate).

That was easy to shrug off when such a future seemed well beyond a lifetime. It no longer does. The Last Evolution isn't Campbell's sharpest word on utopia, but it's the one anyone is free to use. The utopia is in its first chapter, a world where "no productive work was necessary", and the afterword says where Campbell took it next.

A word on the words

The story keeps its 1932 language: "Man" for humanity, "the Year of the Son of Man", "kilads". One speech calls mankind a parasite. It's there whole, because the film keeps every word, and because the story's last page argues with it.

    After the end

    The record ends by handing the argument to you: "So, you who have read, believe as you will. Then think—and maybe, you will change your belief." Here's some of what happened after it was written, to think with.

    What became of Campbell

    Two years later, as "Don A. Stuart", Campbell published "Twilight" (Astounding, November 1934), the story he'd written first and couldn't sell: mankind dwindled and idle, its machines running on perfectly, without purpose (Twilight). The Last Evolution is the triumphant version, and "Twilight" the sad one.

    In 1937 he became editor of Astounding, and he kept the job for 34 years, until he died in 1971. He found and pushed writers such as Isaac Asimov and Robert A. Heinlein, and shaped what fans call the Golden Age of science fiction (Golden Age). His later record is darker. He promoted pseudoscience, Dianetics among it, and his editorials took a hard right-wing line (SFE). In 2019 the award named for him was renamed the Astounding Award (Astounding Award). That history sits alongside the story, not in place of it.

    What became of the science

    The positron was photographed the month the story appeared, and it settled Roal's question the other way. Matter annihilates only with antimatter, an electron with a positron, and a proton and an electron never do (positron, annihilation). The hydrogen "wall" turned out to be the atom's lowest state: there's nothing below it to fall to (Bohr model). The first electronic memories came in the late 1940s (Williams tube), and the machines that think are still being argued about.

    What became of the idea

    Campbell wasn't first. Samuel Butler wondered in 1863 whether machines might evolve to supplant us (Darwin among the Machines). After him came Turing in 1951, expecting that the machines would "take control" (Quote Investigator); I. J. Good's "intelligence explosion" in 1965 (technological singularity); and Hans Moravec's Mind Children in 1988, robots as humanity's heirs (Mind Children).

    Today the argument has two camps. One says succession is coming and needn't be feared; in 2025 the AI pioneer Richard Sutton called it inevitable (Dwarkesh Podcast). The other says it's a risk to be prevented; in 2023 hundreds of researchers and the heads of the major AI labs signed a one-sentence statement that the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority (Statement on AI Risk).

    There's a third worry, older and quieter, and it was Campbell's own: not that the machines fail us, but that they succeed. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes guessed that "the economic problem may be solved" within a hundred years. Then, he wrote, man would face "his real, his permanent problem": how to use his freedom. He thought "with dread" of how the ordinary man would adjust (Keynes). Campbell's Stuart stories gave the same dread a shape: in "Twilight" and the three Machine stories of 1935, machines provide everything, and mankind loses its curiosity and drive (Twilight, SFE, Black Gate). In 2024 Nick Bostrom's Deep Utopia took up the question for an age of AI: if machines do everything better, what makes a life worth living (Nick Bostrom)?

    Campbell's story fits none of these neatly. Its machines are loyal, they grieve, and they fight for their makers, and mankind is killed from outside. Even its utopia, the idle world of its first chapter, ends by invasion before it can go stale. What it asks is the question Roal answers and F-2 hands back: if the heirs are better, should we mind?

    What became of the text

    Amazing reprinted the story in March 1961, and that's the text Project Gutenberg transcribed (eBook #27462). Setting it beside the 1932 pages turned up a 24-word clause and two negatives lost in 1961, and thirteen smaller slips. The film reads the 1932 readings, and the Read page marks each one. Neither printing's copyright was renewed, so the story and Morey's picture are free for anyone to use in the US (first periodical renewals).

    0. The Record

    0:00The textThe title page

    The line over the title names the issue the story first ran in: Amazing Stories for August 1932, pages 414 to 421. The story's words come from its March 1961 reprint in Amazing, the text Project Gutenberg transcribed, and the film reads that text with 16 readings from 1932 put back.

    0:27Who's whoThe narrator

    A machine, the last of its type, writing its memoir 125,000 years after the events it tells. The story doesn't name it until its last pages. The film shows it from the start: the small, rusted torpedo of metal. The coloured spirals of light drifting round it aren't galaxies: they're the beings of Force, "those who are now", the heirs the story is about, and the only full colour in the film.

    0:54WordsMentatype

    Campbell's word, a machine that records thought as a typewriter records keys. The story's last pages call the record "excerpts from certain records on thin sheets of metal", which is how the film draws it.

    1. 2538

    1:241932 scienceSix centuries of machines

    "The Year of the Son of Man" is the machines' A.D. Six centuries back from 2538 is the late 1930s, so the machine age begins in the story's own near future. In 1932 machines could hear (the telephone, radio) and were starting to see (television), but there was no electronic computer anywhere, and Turing's paper defining one was four years away (Turing machine).

    1:55HeirsA world without work

    Campbell's 2538 is a utopia an economist had just imagined. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes guessed that "the economic problem may be solved" within a century, and thought "with dread" of how the ordinary man would meet the freedom (Keynes).

    Campbell came back to this world and soured on it. In "Twilight" (1934) and the Machine stories after it, perfect machines provide everything, and mankind loses its curiosity and drive (Twilight). Here the Outsiders come before anyone finds out.

    2:521932 scienceTen billion in 2100

    When Campbell wrote, the world held about two billion people; it passed two billion in 1927 (World population). His peak, near ten billion around 2100, is close to the UN's projection today: about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, then a slow fall (World Population Prospects 2024).

    3:37The makingThe curl and the ruled line

    The film draws man's imagination as a loose curl of wash and the machine's as a ruled ink line. The branching lattice that follows is the machine's way: three facts, and a world built from them. The pictures are ours; the text describes the two kinds without picturing them.

    2. The Outsiders

    5:10WordsKilads

    The footnote on screen is the story's own. The machines count in twelves, so "a thousand" kilads is 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728, which the footnote calls "about 1/2 mile". A kilad is about a foot and a half, and each ship half a mile long.

    5:391932 scienceFrom Deimos, at a thousand g

    Footnote 2 makes a unit one Earth gravity. A thousand g would kill any crew, and the machine has none. Deimos is the smaller, outer moon of Mars, found by Asaph Hall in 1877 (Deimos).

    7:20The textWhere the picture was printed

    In the magazine, Leo Morey's full-page illustration fell here, on page 415, though it shows a moment from chapter 5. The film saves it for that moment.

    7:41The text24 words put back

    The 1961 reprint lost 24 words here, from "could not withstand it" to "the entire machine", so the ship blew up with nothing to set it off. The film reads the 1932 printing.

    9:071932 scienceThe ladder of power

    Chemical energy, then "blasting atoms" (nuclear), then turning matter wholly into energy. E = mc² said how much energy there was to have, and in the 1920s Eddington and Jeans asked whether the stars shine that way (before Bethe).

    9:27The makingOnly energy has colour

    The world is ink wash on pulp paper, as the magazine's own pictures were, and only energy is coloured, in the colour the text names. That was its "bluish beam".

    3. Life Alone Can Understand Life

    10:12Who's whoRoal and Trest

    The two greatest human scientists. Roal is the dreamer, with the soft voice and the chuckle. Trest is the plain one, the realist. In 2538 people carry numbers as well as names. The narrator says it was there with them, "in memory"; chapter 5 explains how.

    10:51HeirsLife alone can understand life

    The chapter's title. The machines were built to cooperate, and can't imagine destroying merely to possess. It takes a living thing to read another's greed: the first of the story's claims that life and machine think differently.

    11:121932 scienceA furnace turned into a weapon

    Induction furnaces heat metal with alternating magnetic fields, and in 1932 they were new. Edwin Northrup's reached 3,600 degrees in 1931 (Edwin Fitch Northrup). Turning one into a beam is Campbell's step.

    11:40The makingThe voices

    Every voice in the film is made with ElevenLabs, each designed for its part. None is a real person's voice, cloned. The narrator's was chosen for lines like this one: old, precise, and dry.

    13:261932 scienceThe green beams

    They affect chemical action, "retarding it greatly in exothermic actions, speeding greatly endothermic actions" (chapter 4). Life runs on reactions that give off heat, so such a beam would stop it. In 2538 the dead can usually be revived; these beams burn out the nerves, so they can't be.

    15:571932 scienceWells's lesson

    In H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds (1898), the Martians conquer Earth and then die of its germs (The War of the Worlds). Campbell's Outsiders come prepared: their beams kill every living thing down to the microbes, and their own bodies' quick rot shows the machines why.

    17:091932 sciencePluto, the newest planet

    Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto in February 1930, two years before the story, so it was the outermost planet Roal names. It was counted as one until 2006 (Pluto). The film's insert of the planets is ours, for this line.

    4. The Council

    18:21Who's whoThe machines' names

    Type and number. X- are the science machines (X-5638, X-6349, X-6621). C-R-21 is chief of the machine rulers. MX-3401 directs the building of the new brain. The film says them digit by digit, as the machines would.

    19:171932 scienceSix hundred years

    Six hundred years before 2538 is the 1930s. 1932 was the year the atom came apart: the neutron in February; in April, Cockcroft and Walton's machine split lithium, transmuting it, as X-5638 has just done with soil; and in August, the month this story appeared, the positron, the first antimatter (positron).

    20:30The makingThe sound of a machine

    This line set the sound of every machine voice in the film: designed voices, given a light metallic treatment, each family told apart by timbre and pace.

    22:06HeirsImagination by recombination

    "Countless trillions of facts to combine and recombine". Chapter 1 drew the line this machine is meant to cross: machines had "imagination of the ideal sort", sure steps from fact to conclusion, and man the "illogical, brilliant" kind that leaps. This new brain, the one chapter 5 names F-1, is built to imagine by sheer quantity, fed everything its makers knew.

    It reads now like a description of how today's AI models are made. Meta's Llama 3, in 2024, learned from about 15 trillion tokens, fragments of words (Llama). Campbell wrote it before there was an electronic computer anywhere.

    Whether recombination on that scale is imagination is still argued. In 2021 four researchers called such models "stochastic parrots", stitching together forms they'd seen without reference to meaning; others point to what the models work out that was never in the text (Stochastic parrot). The story keeps both sides: F-1, and the F-2 it goes on to make, are more than anything before them, and in chapter 8 F-2 still tells the two men "your swift brains have leapt ahead of us".

    5. The Hydrogen Wall

    24:111932 scienceThe hydrogen wall

    Roal's dream is Niels Bohr's atom of 1913, where the electron falls from orbit to orbit and then stops, for no reason Bohr could give (Bohr model). Quantum mechanics answered it: the lowest state is the lowest standing wave, with nothing below it. The wall is real, but it isn't a wall.

    25:171932 scienceElectric force curves space

    Einstein had made gravity the curvature of space. From 1918 Weyl, Kaluza and Einstein himself tried to do the same for electricity (unified field theories). Roal's leap is the one that programme promised and never made.

    26:59The makingThe sound

    The text names its own sounds, the whine of transmuted air, the welding's sputter, the generators' hum, and those are the effects here. No music plays under the voice. The theme at the chapter heads is led by a theremin, the electronic instrument Leon Theremin invented in 1920 (Theremin).

    27:51The textLeo Morey's page

    The August 1932 issue's illustration, page 415: F-1's tentacles, the newborn in its aura, a man shielding his face. Morey painted 71 covers for Amazing (SFE). The film paints its way up to his picture, then shows the page.

    28:41HeirsThe heir and its maker

    The first heir won't repair its maker. Remember the line in chapter 9.

    29:37Who's whoHow the narrator remembers

    F-2 holds every memory of the machines before it. That's how the narrator, F-2 itself, remembers chapters 1 to 4, from before it was built. The old machine of chapter 0 is this one.

    30:55The textA lost "not"

    The 1961 reprint dropped "not", so the ruler said the opposite of what it meant. It dropped a "don't" from Roal in chapter 3 too. The film reads 1932's.

    6. Ten Thousand Motes

    33:291932 scienceViolet, blue, orange, red

    Hot things glow bluer, and cooler ones redder (Wien's law, 1893), so the screens' run from violet to red reads as their power draining away. The film runs the colours in the text's order.

    34:09The makingInside a giant

    The look inside a giant ship is ours, for this line. The "horny-skinned men" are the Outsiders, in their transparent suits, feeding the generators.

    36:32Words9,633

    Nearly ten thousand came, and 9,633 move on. The machines' whole sacrifice bought fewer than four in a hundred.

    7. The Dome

    37:39HeirsThe children of men

    Fifty-six years later the roboticist Hans Moravec called robots our Mind Children, our successors and heirs (Mind Children). It's Roal's image.

    37:59The makingThe curl

    The curl that rises over Roal is the film's sign for human imagination, from chapter 1. When he dreams, it rises.

    8. Last of Men

    42:10Heirs"A parasite"

    Roal's harshest line, kept whole, as the film keeps every word. It's his argument, and the story's. The last page will ask you to argue back.

    42:37The makingThe ladder

    Life, man, machine, pure force: the steps are Roal's, and the ladder is the film's picture of them.

    43:33HeirsDarwin among the machines

    In 1863 Samuel Butler applied Darwin to machinery, and wondered whether machines might one day supplant us (Darwin among the Machines). Roal turns the idea round: chance made life, and life made what chance never could.

    44:08HeirsLoyal heirs

    Today's word for these machines is aligned: an AI is aligned if it advances the objectives its makers intended (AI alignment). Today's researchers call that an open problem. Campbell takes it for granted. His machines are loyal, they grieve, and they promise to complete "the dreams you dreamt". Man's end comes from outside, not from them.

    Eight years later Campbell helped write the rules for such machines. Asimov credited him with the Three Laws of Robotics, from a talk between them on 23 December 1940; Campbell said Asimov had them in mind already, and only needed to state them. They first appeared together in "Runaround", in 1942 (Three Laws).

    Not every heir here is loyal: wait for chapter 9.

    45:45The makingLogic and intuition

    "The deadly logic of the machine, inspired by the leaping intuition of man": the ruled line and the curl from chapter 1, together for the first time, in the forces that build the plant. It's what Roal's hint gives F-2, a leap it couldn't have made alone.

    9. Pure Force

    48:53HeirsAn heir that turns

    As F-2 put aside F-1, this new thing puts aside F-2, and it shoots. It's the story's one glimpse of a successor that isn't loyal, and the power plant was ready for it.

    51:36HeirsNot necessary to destroy

    The heir is kinder than the killers of its makers. The Outsiders are sent home, not destroyed.

    10. Go, and Do Not Return

    54:04The makingLocked

    In this film the settings hold still and everything that lives or acts boils, its lines redrawn twelve times a second. Once the beam has passed, the flagship stops boiling too: the only ship in the film to go still. Nothing in it can change.

    11. Five Great Years

    54:431932 scienceFive Great Years

    Five to 125,000 years makes a Great Year about 25,000, close to the classical Great Year: one turn of the equinoxes' precession, about 25,800 years (Great Year). The machines keep an astronomers' calendar.

    The record goes back in time, to the reader's own day. The story presents itself as that record, printed in a magazine in 1932. The film carries it down a tunnel of light.

    55:57Who's who"I, F-2"

    It has just said so: the narrator is F-2, the machine born in chapter 5, first to use the Ultimate Energy, and last of its kind.
